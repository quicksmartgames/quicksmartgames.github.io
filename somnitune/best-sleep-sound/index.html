Which sleep sound works best for you — and how to find out

Most people asking this want a ranking. There isn't one worth giving, because the research that exists describes averages and you are not an average. What can be done is narrower and more useful: say honestly which categories have real evidence behind them, then set up a test you can run in your own bed.

Last updated September 2026 · about 8 minutes

First, how common this is

Sleeping to sound is not a niche habit. In a 2023 survey of 2,005 US adults by the American Academy of Sleep Medicine, 47% said they sleep with a fan [10]. A November 2024 survey of 1,000 US adults by Talker Research put the share who use sound to fall asleep at around 38% [11].

Among people who pick a noise colour, the preferences are more evenly split than the marketing suggests. In a Sleep Doctor survey reported by AARP: green 40.1%, white 38.1%, pink 14.7%, brown 13.8% [12]. Brown noise dominates social media and comes last of the four in that sample — a reminder that loudness of discussion and usefulness are unrelated quantities.

The categories, ranked by evidence — not by popularity

Music: the only category with decent evidence

This is the finding that surprises people. A 2022 Cochrane review, Listening to music for insomnia in adults, pooled 13 randomised trials with 1,007 participants and concluded that listening to music probably improves sleep quality, with moderate-certainty evidence — an effect of roughly one standard deviation on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index [4].

Moderate certainty is not a thunderclap, but set it beside the noise literature and the gap is enormous. Music is the best-supported thing you can put in your ears at bedtime, and it is the category sleep-sound apps tend to treat as an afterthought behind the rain loops.

Two caveats that matter. Cochrane found the benefit on self-reported sleep quality; the evidence on how fast people fell asleep and how long they slept was weaker. And "music" in these trials generally meant calm, slow, self-selected listening — not whatever is on shuffle.

Continuous noise: very low quality evidence, and a known cost

White, pink, brown, fan, rain, aeroplane cabin — all the same family. The 2021 systematic review in Sleep Medicine Reviews gathered 38 studies and graded the quality of evidence that continuous noise improves sleep as very low [1].

And in a 2026 polysomnography study of 25 people published in Sleep, 50 dB pink noise reduced REM sleep by about 19 minutes a night, while earplugs did better at blunting intermittent environmental noise without that cost [2]. If the thing keeping you awake is a noisy street, the unglamorous fix currently has better numbers than the app does.

Not-a-sound: worth knowing about

If you are comparing bedtime interventions, it is only fair to include one that isn't audio at all. In a 2015 randomised trial in JAMA Internal Medicine, 49 older adults with sleep disturbance did mindfulness meditation, and the between-group effect size on the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index was 0.89 [5]. Small trial, specific population — but a large effect, and free.

The honest framing: sound is convenient, passive and pleasant. It is not obviously the most effective bedtime intervention available, and nobody selling sounds has an incentive to tell you that.

Why the ranking still doesn't answer your question

Because every row above is an average over people, and averages can hide opposite effects.

The cleanest published illustration is a 2024 meta-analysis in the Journal of the American Academy of Child & Adolescent Psychiatry: 13 studies, 335 young people, white and pink noise during task performance. In youth with ADHD, a small benefit. In non-ADHD controls, the same noise impaired performance [3]. Different people, same sound, opposite direction.

That was attention, not sleep, so don't over-read it. But it demonstrates the structural point: if a pooled result can be positive for one group and negative for another, then "studies show X helps" is not a statement about you. It's a statement about a distribution you might sit at either end of.

The useful question is not "what is the best sleep sound?" It is "which of the sounds I would actually use gets me to sleep fastest?" That one has an answer, and you can measure it.

How to run the test properly

A self-experiment is easy to run badly. Five things decide whether the result means anything.

1. Change one thing

Keep bedtime, room temperature, caffeine cut-off and screen habits as constant as you can manage, and vary only the sound. If you change the sound the same week you start going to bed an hour earlier, you have learnt nothing about the sound.

2. Give each sound at least a week

Sleep onset is wildly variable night to night. A single hard day moves it more than any audio track will. One night proves nothing; a week per sound starts to be readable; several weeks is better. Rotate rather than testing a sound once and moving on.

3. Pick the right outcome

For the sound question, time to fall asleep is the outcome that matters most, because that's the window when the sound is actually playing. Total sleep and number of wake-ups are worth watching too — especially if you're masking a noisy environment, where the value should show up in fewer disturbances rather than a faster drop-off.

4. Know how good your measurement is

This is where most self-experiments quietly fail. Memory is a terrible instrument: nobody accurately recalls how long they lay awake. A wearable is much better. A phone alone is a real estimate but a rougher one, and time-to-fall-asleep is specifically the measure phones handle worst — worth reading before you trust a number.

5. Include a control

Put silence in the rotation. Weeks with no sound at all are the comparison that tells you whether the sound is doing anything, or whether you would have slept the same either way. Without it you can only rank sounds against each other, never against nothing.

Two practical notes

Volume. Keep it low enough that it covers what you needed covered and no more. A 2014 Pediatrics study of 14 infant sound machines found all of them exceeded the 50 dBA nursery limit at 30 cm, and three exceeded 85 dBA [6]. The same carelessness is easy on a phone speaker.

All night or a timer? If you're masking a noisy street, continuous makes sense. If you're not, a timer means the sound stops once it has done its job, which also avoids leaving 50 dB running through the REM-heavy second half of the night.
