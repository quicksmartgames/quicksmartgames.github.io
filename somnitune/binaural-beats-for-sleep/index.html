Do binaural beats help you sleep?

Binaural beats are the "delta wave" tracks that fill sleep playlists. The idea is neat: two slightly different tones, one per ear, and your brain is said to follow the difference down into sleep. The trials are small and mixed, and the one large test I found says the beat itself may not be what matters.

Last updated November 2026 · about 6 minutes

This page covers what a binaural beat is, what the sleep studies actually did, and how to find out whether it works for you without trusting a playlist title.

What a binaural beat is

Play 200 Hz in your left ear and 202 Hz in your right, and what you perceive is a faint pulse at the difference: 2 Hz, two slow throbs per second. Neither ear gets a pulse; the beat is produced in the brain from the two inputs. That is why it only exists in stereo headphones. Through a speaker or a mono earbud the two tones mix in the air and you hear something else, an ordinary beating sound, or nothing special.

The sleep claim is that a beat at 1–4 Hz (the delta range) or 4–8 Hz (theta) nudges brain activity toward the same rhythm, which is called entrainment, and that this makes falling asleep easier.

Does the brain follow the beat?

That is the foundation, and it is shakier than the playlists suggest. A 2023 systematic review in PLoS One looked at 14 studies of binaural beats and brain oscillations. Five supported entrainment, eight contradicted it, one was mixed. The authors called the results overall inconsistent and blamed partly the very different methods across studies [1].

What the sleep studies found

There is no meta-analysis of sleep outcomes that I could find. A 2026 systematic review of music and binaural-beat interventions in young adults included ten trials and concluded the evidence may point to benefit, while saying that heterogeneity and small samples limit the certainty and that properly powered randomised trials are needed [2]. These are the individual studies I checked:

StudyWhoWhat it didResult
Lee 2024, Sleep [3]10 healthy adultsTwo nights in a lab, beats of 0–3 Hz versus no soundFaster sleep onset with beats
Fan 2024, Sci Rep [4]12 healthy adults90-minute daytime naps, 0.25 Hz and 1 Hz beats versus no soundFaster slow-wave sleep with 0.25 Hz; no entrainment detected
Lin 2024, Geriatr Gerontol Int [5]64 older adults with poor sleep14 days of 20-minute sessions, music with beats versus the same music withoutBetter sleep quality with beats
Shumov 2023, Zh Nevrol Psikhiatr [6]221 people at homeBinaural beats versus monaural beats versus the same tone without a beatNo difference in time to fall asleep

Read it as a pattern, not a verdict:

  • The positive studies are small (10, 12, 64 people) and short. Two of them compared beats with silence, which cannot separate "the beat helped" from "a steady sound helped."
  • The Fan nap study found faster slow-wave sleep but no sign of entrainment, so even there the mechanism is unexplained. It was funded by a company whose employees are authors, and a related patent application has been filed, which is worth knowing though it does not make the data wrong.
  • The largest test is the Russian study with 221 participants. When the comparison was a binaural beat against a monaural beat (the same pulse, but audible without headphones) or a plain tone, sleep latency did not differ (p = 0.21) [6]. That is the control the others lack, and it points to a steady sound doing the work.
  • The care-home trial is the strongest of the positive ones, with a music-only control, but it is one trial, in one population, over two weeks.

The honest summary

Binaural beats have not been shown to help sleep beyond what a plain steady sound does. A few small trials are encouraging, one large test is not, and the brain-entrainment idea behind them is itself contested. They are low-risk to try, and no more than that.

If you try them

  • Stereo headphones or earbuds only. Speakers cannot deliver a binaural beat; the effect needs one tone per ear.
  • Keep the volume low. These tracks are quiet tones, not something to hear loudly for hours in your ear canal.
  • Use a timer. Sleeping with earbuds is a comfort question as much as a sound one, and the studies above used short sessions.
  • Judge it by your own nights, not by the track name. "Delta" on a thumbnail tells you the beat frequency, not whether it works for you.
